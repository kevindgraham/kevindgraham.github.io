About

Written from the classroom, not about it

I teach digital media to high school students in the Dallas area. Everything in this book started as a problem I had to solve at 7:15 in the morning — students using tools nobody had taught them to use, no district playbook yet, and a class to teach in fifteen minutes.

I'm a career and technical education teacher, a photographer and videographer, and the author of Finally Understand AI. I hold a master's in communication and Texas certifications in technology applications and technology education. Most of what I do outside the classroom is translating fast-moving technology into plain language for people who are tired of feeling behind.

That's the whole promise of this book and this page: practical support from someone who is in a classroom, this year, figuring it out alongside you.

FAQ

Questions teachers ask me

Is using AI automatically cheating?

No. Cheating is submitting work that misrepresents what you know and did — that was true before AI. What matters is whether the assignment said AI was allowed. That's why the book marks every assignment Red, Yellow, or Green: clarity before consequences.

Should students have to disclose AI use?

Yes, whenever it's allowed. And disclosure should never cost them points — the moment honesty is penalized, students hide it instead. Hiding is the actual integrity problem.

Can I put student work into an AI tool?

Not into tools your district hasn't approved. Anything that identifies a student — names, grades, IEP details, behavior records — stays out. Districts approve tools because contracts require the vendor to protect that data. That's the protection, not the tool's privacy policy.

Are AI detectors reliable?

Not reliable enough to be your only evidence. They produce false positives that fall hardest on second-language writers and students who write in the structured way we taught them. Use a detector result as a reason to look closer, never as a verdict — and never accuse a student on a score alone.

What if I suspect a student misused AI?

Have a conversation, not a trial. Ask them to walk you through the work. A student who did it can, easily. The book includes a four-step script, and it ends with learning rather than a gotcha.

Can younger students use these tools?

Check the age requirement first — many tools require 13+, some 18+, and the district version often has different rules than the free consumer version. When in doubt, the teacher drives the tool and students do the thinking.

My district has no AI policy. Where do I start?

You're not unusual — most teachers are in that position. Start with your own classroom: a syllabus statement, the traffic-light system, and the two hard rules (no identifiable student data in AI tools; no accusations based on a detector alone). Then ask your administration the questions in Chapter 10.

Can I use these materials for my whole campus or district?

Yes. Everything on this page is free to reproduce for classroom, campus, or district training. If you'd like the book itself for a team, ask about bulk orders above.
